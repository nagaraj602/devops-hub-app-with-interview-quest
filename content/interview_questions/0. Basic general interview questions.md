## Section 1: General, Process & Behavioral Questions

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>1. What was your team size and structure?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"The overall project team had 12 members. We had 8 developers, 2 QA engineers, and 2 DevOps engineers. Responsibilities were clearly distributed, and from the DevOps side, I was primarily involved in CI/CD, AWS infrastructure, Docker containerization, Terraform, deployments, and troubleshooting. I worked closely with developers and QA during release cycles."

*Note: Interviewers use this to understand your actual level of exposure. Do not exaggerate the team size, as follow-up questions will demand consistency.*

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>2. How many DevOps engineers were there?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"There were 2 DevOps engineers. We shared responsibilities across CI/CD maintenance, cloud infrastructure provisioning, automated deployments, monitoring, and production support. While my colleague focused heavily on deep monitoring configurations, I was primarily involved in pipeline architecture and infrastructure-as-code using Terraform."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>3. What was your exact responsibility?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"My primary responsibilities were maintaining the CI/CD pipelines, writing and updating Terraform configurations for our AWS infrastructure, containerizing microservices with Docker, supporting Kubernetes deployments, monitoring infrastructure health, and troubleshooting deployment or configuration issues."

**Answer:**
"Rather than just maintaining what was there, I personally created the VPC and networking modules in Terraform, configured the remote state with S3 and DynamoDB locking, wrote the EC2 and security-group resources, and integrated these Terraform stages directly into our Jenkins deployment pipeline."

*Note: The interviewer is trying to separate "I worked on this project" from "I watched someone else work on this project." Be specific.*

**Answer:**
"I was primarily supporting 3 core microservices: User Authentication, Payment Processing, and a Notification Engine. My DevOps responsibilities across these included maintaining their distinct CI/CD pipelines, provisioning their supporting infrastructure, managing deployments, and configuring their monitoring dashboards."

**Answer:**
"We maintained 4 distinct environments: DEV, QA, UAT, and PROD. The deployment to DEV was fully automated upon merging to the development branch, while QA and UAT had automated triggers but required manual testing sign-offs. PROD had strict manual approval gates and validation steps before any deployment."

**Answer:**
"The DevOps team managed the underlying infrastructure and the deployment mechanics across all environments. Developers primarily worked within and supported the DEV environment. The QA team owned the testing lifecycle in QA and UAT. Production changes were strictly controlled through a formal release and approval process, executed by DevOps."

</details>

<summary><strong>8. How many servers did you manage?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"We didn't manage servers as static pets. The infrastructure was entirely codified through AWS and Terraform. At any given time, we ran approximately 50 EC2 instances across our 4 environments. However, that number fluctuated dynamically because our production and UAT environments utilized Auto Scaling Groups based on traffic demands."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>9. Did you have a separate DevOps team?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Yes. Development and QA were separate functional units. The DevOps team operated cross-functionally to support them, handling all cloud infrastructure, CI/CD pipeline engineering, release deployments, and operational infrastructure support."

</details>

<summary><strong>10. Did you work directly with developers?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Yes, daily. DevOps worked closely with developers during the initial CI/CD integration for new features, debugging failed builds, optimizing Dockerfiles, configuring application environment variables, and troubleshooting network or architectural issues during release activities."

</details>

<details markdown="1">

**Answer:**
"The DevOps team managed the AWS infrastructure. I was personally responsible for creating and maintaining the Terraform configurations for the networking and compute layers I worked on. All infrastructure changes were managed through Git, requiring a pull request, a plan review, and a peer approval before being applied to AWS."

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>12. Who created and managed the Jenkins pipelines?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I created and maintained the Jenkins pipelines for our CI/CD processes. I wrote the `Jenkinsfile` stages which handled source checkout, building the application, running unit tests and SonarQube quality checks, building the Docker image, pushing the image to ECR, and ultimately triggering the deployment."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>13. How many Jenkins pipelines did you manage?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I directly managed 6 primary pipelines—essentially a CI (Continuous Integration) and a CD (Continuous Deployment) pipeline for each of the 3 microservices I supported. While they followed a shared modular structure via Jenkins Shared Libraries, the application-specific build and deployment variables varied."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>14. Did developers have access to Jenkins?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Developers had read-only access to view pipeline logs and the ability to manually trigger lower-environment (DEV/QA) deployments. However, sensitive production deployment permissions were heavily restricted through Jenkins Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) and required manager-level approval controls within the pipeline."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>15. Who handled production and deployments?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Production operations and deployments were exclusively handled by the DevOps team. We had defined Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for zero-downtime deployments, monitoring application health post-release, and handling infrastructure incidents. For critical production changes, we adhered strictly to the Change Advisory Board (CAB) approval process."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>16. Were you involved in production deployments?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Yes. I actively executed production deployments via our CI/CD pipeline. The process involved ensuring all code passed required checks and approvals. Once approved, I triggered the Jenkins CD pipeline to deploy the immutable artifact to production. I then monitored the rollout logs, metrics, and health checks to ensure stability before signing off on the release."

*(If you didn't have prod access, use this instead: "I mainly managed lower environments and supported senior engineers during production rollouts. I fully understand the production deployment flow, but I did not independently push the final production deployment button.")*

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>17. Who approved production deployments and had the final authority?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"While the DevOps team performed the technical execution of the deployment, the final authority to release to production belonged to the Product Owner or Engineering Manager. They provided the business sign-off through our change-management system (e.g., Jira/ServiceNow) before we could technically authorize the Jenkins pipeline to proceed."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>18. Who had production access?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Production access was strictly limited to authorized senior engineers and on-call DevOps personnel. Access was governed by the Principle of Least Privilege using AWS IAM roles and RBAC. We used AWS Systems Manager (SSM) Session Manager for necessary CLI access rather than distributing SSH keys, ensuring all actions were logged and auditable."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>19. Could you independently create an EC2 instance in production?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Technically, my IAM role might have permitted it, but I would never create production infrastructure manually outside of our established process. Any infrastructure change must be written in Terraform, committed to version control, peer-reviewed, and applied via our CI/CD pipeline to prevent configuration drift and maintain an auditable history."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>20. What happens if a developer asks you to bypass the pipeline and manually deploy?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I would decline and explain that bypassing the pipeline eliminates our automated testing, security checks, and traceability, inevitably leading to configuration drift. We must use the standard deployment process. If it is a genuine, critical outage requiring immediate manual mitigation, I would follow our documented emergency 'break-glass' procedure, apply the fix, and ensure the manual changes are immediately backported into code."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>21. What if your manager asks you to deploy something you know is risky?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I wouldn't blindly deploy it. It is my job to protect production. I would clearly articulate the risk, provide technical evidence (e.g., failing tests, missing dependencies, infrastructure bottlenecks), and suggest a safer alternative. If the business accepts the risk and the change is still explicitly approved, I would ensure we follow the change-management process and have a verified rollback plan in place before proceeding."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>22. Did you have an on-call rotation?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Yes, we had a formal on-call rotation using PagerDuty. During my assigned rotation, I was responsible for responding to high-severity production alerts, assessing the impact, mitigating the issue if possible, or escalating to specific domain experts (like a DBA or Senior Backend Engineer) if the root cause was deep within the application code."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>23. Who handled incidents?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"The DevOps team owned the initial technical triage. Depending on the nature of the issue, we collaborated with software engineers, database admins, or network engineers. 

My standard flow is:
**Alert -> Assess Impact -> Check Logs/Metrics -> Identify Recent Changes -> Mitigate Impact -> Determine Root Cause -> Apply Permanent Fix -> Conduct Post-Incident Review.**"

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>24. Who would you contact if production went down at 2 AM?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"As the on-call engineer, my first step is to open the incident runbook and investigate the alert using our observability stack. I prioritize restoring service. If I am unable to mitigate the issue within our defined SLA (e.g., 15-30 minutes) or if the issue requires system credentials I don't possess, I escalate immediately to the designated senior engineer or the specific application team on-call via our escalation policy."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>25. What happens if your senior is unavailable?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I would follow the documented runbook, utilizing logs, metrics, and recent deployment history to attempt a safe mitigation (such as rolling back the last deployment or scaling resources). If I remain blocked and cannot resolve it safely, I would escalate to the next person in the defined escalation chain—typically an Engineering Manager or Director. I will not execute high-risk, unverified manual changes in production just because a senior isn't answering."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>26. You don't know the answer to a production problem. What will you do?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I never guess or execute random commands in production. I systematically gather evidence: application logs, infrastructure metrics, monitoring dashboards, and the commit history for recent changes. If I can, I try to reproduce the anomaly in UAT. If I am still blocked, I package all the evidence I've gathered and escalate to a senior engineer. Knowing how to efficiently investigate, gather data, and escalate is much more valuable than pretending to know everything."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>27. What happens if you make a mistake in production?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I immediately acknowledge it. Hiding a mistake is infinitely worse than making one. My absolute first priority is minimizing customer impact—I will revert the change, restore from a backup, or apply a mitigation strategy. Once the service is stable, I investigate exactly why the mistake occurred. Finally, I implement preventive measures—such as stricter CI/CD validations, automated testing, or tighter IAM permissions—so the system prevents a human from making that exact error again."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>28. Why do we need DevOps? Developers can deploy themselves.</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Developers absolutely can and should deploy their own code, and DevOps shouldn't exist just to act as a deployment gatekeeper. The true value of DevOps is engineering a reliable, secure, and automated delivery system. We standardize infrastructure, embed security, configure observability, and build automated rollback mechanisms. This empowers developers to deliver code rapidly and independently without introducing operational risks into production."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>29. What is the difference between DevOps and SRE?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"DevOps is primarily a cultural philosophy and a set of practices focused on breaking down silos, improving collaboration, and automating the software delivery lifecycle (CI/CD). 

Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) is a specific engineering discipline that applies software engineering principles to IT operations. SRE focuses strictly on reliability, utilizing quantifiable metrics like Service Level Objectives (SLOs), Service Level Indicators (SLIs), and Error Budgets to balance feature velocity with system stability. In short, 'class SRE implements DevOps'."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>30. Why should I hire you as a DevOps Engineer?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"You should hire me because I don't view DevOps as just a shopping list of tools. I deeply understand the complete lifecycle from source code to production availability. While I am highly proficient with Git, Jenkins, Docker, Terraform, AWS, and Kubernetes, more importantly, I understand *why* and *when* to use them. I am highly comfortable troubleshooting complex, ambiguous problems systematically, and I take extreme ownership of ensuring systems are resilient, secure, and maintainable."

</details>

## Section 2: Technical & Scenario-Based Questions

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>31. Tell me about a production issue you handled.</strong></summary>

**Answer:**

*(If you have actual experience, detail it clearly. If your experience is primarily project-based, use this mature approach:)*

"I haven't personally been the lead on a massive live production outage yet, so I won't claim that I have. However, I have simulated and resolved critical production-like scenarios in my environments. For example, I handled a scenario where a deployment failed due to database connectivity exhaustion. 

My approach to any incident is standard: 
1. Assess the blast radius (is it affecting customers?).
2. Check monitoring, metrics, and application logs.
3. Identify the last deployed change or configuration update.
4. Mitigate the impact immediately (e.g., scale up connection pools or rollback the deployment).
5. Investigate the root cause offline and write a post-mortem to prevent recurrence."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>32. What happens if your deployment fails in production?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"First, I determine if the failed deployment is causing active customer impact. I check the CI/CD pipeline status, application logs, health checks, and system metrics. If the new version is actively degrading the system, my immediate priority is to **restore service** via an automated rollback to the previous stable version. 

Only after the service is restored and stable do I investigate the root cause of the failure. Once identified and fixed, I would introduce preventative measures—like stricter deployment gates, improved pre-flight health checks, or better integration tests—before attempting the deployment again."
*Key Concept: Restore service first, root cause second.*

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>33. You used Jenkins. What happens when a deployment fails?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"When a deployment stage fails in Jenkins, the pipeline immediately halts to prevent further rollout. 

The flow looks like this:
**Build -> Test -> Push Artifact -> Deploy -> Health Check -> FAILURE.**

Upon failure, Jenkins triggers an alert via Slack/Email. If we are using a blue/green or canary deployment strategy, the load balancer simply stops routing traffic to the failed instances, and the 'blue' (old) environment continues serving users. If it’s a rolling update, the pipeline is configured to automatically trigger a rollback script to revert the infrastructure to the last known good artifact."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>34. Your Kubernetes pod is running, but the application isn't accessible. What do you check?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I troubleshoot systematically, working from the inside out (Layer 7 down to Layer 3/4):

1. **Pod Level:** I check `kubectl logs <pod-name>` to ensure the application inside the container actually started without crashing and is listening on the expected port. I also verify Readiness and Liveness probes (`kubectl describe pod <pod-name>`).
2. **Service Level:** I check if the Kubernetes `Service` is selecting the right pods (`kubectl get endpoints <service-name>`). I verify the `port` and `targetPort` mappings match the container port.
3. **Ingress/Network Level:** If it's exposed externally, I check the `Ingress` resource rules and the Load Balancer status.
4. **Security Level:** I verify Kubernetes `NetworkPolicies` and AWS Security Groups to ensure traffic isn't being dropped at the network layer."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>35. Tell me about the most difficult Kubernetes issue you solved.</strong></summary>

**Answer:**

*(Structure your answer using this framework)*
*   **Symptom:** "We had random pods continuously crash-looping under heavy load."
*   **Investigation:** "I noticed CPU throttling, but memory looked fine. I used `kubectl describe pod` and checked the exact exit codes."
*   **Root Cause:** "The application was hitting OOMKilled (Exit Code 137), but our monitoring was aggregating metrics too slowly to catch the micro-spikes in memory usage. Also, the CPU limits were set too aggressively, causing severe throttling during startup."
*   **Fix:** "I adjusted the resource `requests` and `limits`, removing the CPU limits entirely (as is best practice in modern K8s) and increasing the memory limit to accommodate the spikes."
*   **Prevention:** "I implemented tighter Prometheus alerting on memory utilization and configured a Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA) to scale based on custom metrics rather than just CPU."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>36. Terraform state gets deleted. What happens?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Terraform state is the absolute source of truth that maps your configuration code to real-world cloud resources. If the state file is deleted, Terraform suffers amnesia—it no longer knows that the AWS VPC or EC2 instances it created exist. If you run `terraform apply` without the state, it will attempt to recreate all those resources from scratch, causing massive conflicts or downtime."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>37. You used Terraform. How did you handle state?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"To prevent state loss or corruption, local state files are strictly prohibited in our workflow. 

Here is how I handle it:
1. **Remote Backend:** I store the state centrally in an AWS S3 bucket.
2. **Locking:** I use an Amazon DynamoDB table for state locking to prevent concurrent CI/CD pipeline runs from corrupting the state simultaneously.
3. **Versioning:** S3 versioning is enabled, so if the state is accidentally corrupted or deleted, I can immediately roll back to the previous known-good state file.
4. **Security:** The S3 bucket is tightly restricted via IAM, encrypted at rest with KMS, and blocks all public access, as state files can contain sensitive plaintext secrets."

*(If asked: "Would you run `terraform import` immediately?" -> "No. First, I would attempt to recover the state from S3 version history. `terraform import` is a last resort if the state is completely unrecoverable.")*

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>38. Your EC2 CPU is 100%. What will you do?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I would never immediately reboot the instance, as that destroys volatile log data and evidence. 

1. **Investigate:** I SSH in (via Session Manager) and use `top`, `htop`, or `ps` to identify exactly which process is consuming the CPU. I also review CloudWatch metrics to see when the spike started.
2. **Correlate:** I check if the spike correlates with a recent deployment, a cron job, or a sudden surge in user traffic.
3. **Mitigate:** If it's a legitimate traffic spike, I ensure Auto Scaling is triggering properly (Horizontal scaling). If it's a runaway application process, I restart the specific service.
4. **Prevent:** I document the findings and work with developers to optimize the application or adjust our scaling thresholds to trigger earlier."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>39. What would you do if the production database becomes unavailable?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"1. **Scope and Impact:** I immediately check if the issue is a complete database outage or an application-layer connection exhaustion issue.
2. **Investigation:** I check AWS RDS metrics (CPU, Connections, Storage), security group changes, and application error logs.
3. **Mitigation:** If it's an underlying hardware/infrastructure failure, I verify if our Multi-AZ failover has triggered. If it hasn't, I follow the runbook to initiate a manual failover to the standby replica.
4. **Resolution:** My absolute priority is getting the application reconnected to a healthy database. Deep root-cause analysis (e.g., slow queries locking tables) happens after the service is restored."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>40. Design a highly available application on AWS.</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"A highly available architecture requires eliminating single points of failure across all layers:
*   **DNS:** Amazon Route 53 routes traffic globally with health checks.
*   **Entry Point:** An Application Load Balancer (ALB) distributed across at least 3 Availability Zones (AZs).
*   **Compute:** Amazon EC2 instances (or ECS/EKS containers) deployed via an Auto Scaling Group spanning 3 AZs. If one AZ goes down, the ASG provisions new capacity in the healthy zones.
*   **Database:** Amazon RDS configured with Multi-AZ deployment. Data is synchronously replicated to a standby instance in a different AZ for automatic failover.
*   **Storage:** Static assets and media stored in Amazon S3, served globally via CloudFront (CDN).
*   **Observability:** CloudWatch monitoring all layers, triggering scaling events and SNS alerts automatically."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>41. Why shouldn't we just use `latest` for Docker images?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"Because `latest` is a mutable tag. It does not guarantee idempotency. If I deploy `myapp:latest` today, and a developer pushes a breaking change to `latest` tomorrow, Kubernetes will pull the new, broken image if a pod restarts—even though my Kubernetes deployment manifest hasn't changed. 

This causes unpredictable deployments and makes rollbacks incredibly dangerous. I always use explicit semantic versioning (e.g., `v1.4.2`), commit SHA, or the immutable SHA256 image digest. This ensures absolute traceability and predictable rollbacks."

</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><strong>42. Why did you choose Jenkins instead of GitHub Actions?</strong></summary>

**Answer:**
"I evaluate tools based on organizational requirements, not personal dogmatism. 

Jenkins is highly extensible, cloud-agnostic, and works exceptionally well in enterprise environments where we need complex customizations, deep integrations with on-prem/legacy internal systems, and granular RBAC. 

GitHub Actions is fantastic for its simplicity, managed infrastructure, and native integration if the code is already hosted on GitHub. I would choose Jenkins for complex, highly customized enterprise pipelines, and GitHub Actions for modern, cloud-native projects heavily utilizing the GitHub ecosystem."

</details>

## Appendix: The Golden Rule for Project Preparation

If an interviewer suspects a candidate has memorized tools rather than gained real experience, they will aggressively probe for project specifics. 

For **every** project listed on your resume, you must be able to confidently articulate these 10 facts without hesitation:

1. **Team size:** (e.g., 12 people total)
2. **Number of DevOps engineers:** (e.g., 2 engineers)
3. **Your exact responsibility:** (e.g., CI/CD, Terraform, AWS config)
4. **Application architecture:** (e.g., 3 Microservices, Node.js + PostgreSQL)
5. **Number/type of environments:** (e.g., 4: DEV, QA, UAT, PROD)
6. **CI/CD process:** (e.g., Git -> Jenkins -> SonarQube -> Docker -> ECR -> EKS)
7. **Deployment process:** (e.g., Automated DEV, manual approval for PROD)
8. **Production ownership:** (e.g., DevOps team owned final execution)
9. **On-call/incident process:** (e.g., PagerDuty rotation, specific escalation paths)
10. **One real troubleshooting example:** (e.g., OOMKilled pods, exhausted DB connections, or Terraform state locking issues)
